# Data Analysis Issues
The fundamental goal of the experiment is simply to model the motion of the plate. And the thought process in trying to do this is trying to find the frequencies where the sugar produces the most distinct, visual patterns when vibrated. We figured that this would be where the plate is resonant, and that can be approximated mathematically via simulations, be determined by the loudest frequencies that a microphone detects in the frequency range of interest, or could be determined purely visually and auditorily. So we implemented each of these methods throughout the range to try and find where these resonances are. However, we ended up with conflicting results as to where resonance truly is. The first problem is that the simulations gave a lot of distinct patterns, often more than what we actually found on the plate. Even if we could somewhat make out the pattern we produced at the corresponding frequency, it might not be very distinct, even if we sweep through the nearby range to see if maybe the predicted frequency is slightly off. So we can't just look at every frequency that the simulation predicts, we have to find those frequencies ourselves. We tried selecting out the predicted natural modes that would likely produce the strongest results when driven at the center, but these results weren't particularly distinct or accurate. With the microphone setup we we're pretty successful overall in finding distinct patterns, and once we did, then we would compare those to the simulation at those frequencies, as well as the nearby frequencies that the simulation said would produce similar patterns. We found that our results at the frequencies we determined from the microphone sweep gave much more distinct patterns than the ones that the simulation predicted. Some of these frequencies were similar, but some were noticeably different:

From the table you can see that 4 of the predictions were close, but 4 were also fairly off. It's not the case that there's some consistent amount of Hz that our predicted frequencies are off by compared to the simulated predictions. All of this leaves us with the question, what makes a certain frequency the most likely to be a natural frequency? and furthermore, are natural frequencies even giving the most distinct data? Using the microphone method, it seems like we generally got the best patterns, but our later results, particularly the 684.41 Hz result and the 724.19 Hz result got messy. The overall shape was still correct, but the nodal lines became a lot more difficult to make out. At one point we were testing to see if we could visually find a harmonic series, and in doing so, we found a very distinct pattern for 600 Hz, one that our simulation didn't predict. In our previous experiment, we found two distinct patterns at 604 Hz and 675 Hz that weren't predicted. This wasn't with as good of a setup, but generally the data still looked roughly like the simulations did, so this is still meaningful. In discussing the experimental results I was heavily focused on comparing to the simulations, however, this may have been a mistake. The simulation can either predict a natural frequency that didn't seem to exist, or miss a pattern that was very distinct. The latter could be explained by distinct patterns not always being right at the resonance frequency in some cases, though the former is more difficult to explain. I've realized in making all of these simulations that it would have been better to create contour plots instead. This way we could see how strong the vibration is meant to be at certain spots, and therefore how strong the nodal lines are likely to be at each location quantitatively. I didn't think that this would matter since at the predicted natural frequencies, we should be getting the strongest nodal lines anyways, but this doesn't seem to always be the case. It's also worth noting that in determining which natural modes to compare to, we basically just looked at which simulations seemed like they'd be most effectively produced via our center driven setup, but we could have mis-predicted. Perhaps the simulation was still useful when comparing our data to it where we already knew that we got a good pattern at that frequency, but using it to find what frequencies to use doesn't seem very effective. With all of this in mind, our most useful data set is the microphone data, with corresponding simulations that are still potentially useful, but should be used more as a tool to determine what the perfectly distinct pattern might look like around that particular frequency. One interesting pattern to consider is that our simulations tend to be the least accurate compared to the data as we get to the higher frequencies. This is most evident in the three examples I used previously in regards to distinct patterns that our simulations didn't predict. These values were all above 600 Hz.
