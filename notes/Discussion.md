# Data Analysis Issues
The fundamental goal of the experiment is simply to model the motion of the plate. And the thought process in trying to do this is trying to find the frequencies where the sugar produces the most distinct, visual patterns when vibrated. We figured that this would be where the plate is resonant, and that can be approximated mathematically via simulations, be determined by the loudest frequencies that a microphone detects in the frequency range of interest, or could be determined purely visually and auditorily. So we implemented each of these methods throughout the range to try and find where these resonances are. However, we ended up with conflicting results as to where resonance truly is. The first problem is that the simulations gave a lot of distinct patterns, often more than what we actually found on the plate. Even if we could somewhat make out the pattern we produced at the corresponding frequency, it might not be very distinct, even if we sweep through the nearby range to see if maybe the predicted frequency is slightly off. So we can't just look at every frequency that the simulation predicts, we have to find those frequencies ourselves. We tried selecting out the predicted natural modes that would likely produce the strongest results when driven at the center, but these results weren't particularly distinct or accurate. With the microphone setup we we're pretty successful overall in finding distinct patterns, and once we did, then we would compare those to the simulation at those frequencies, as well as the nearby frequencies that the simulation said would produce similar patterns. We found that our results at the frequencies we determined from the microphone sweep gave much more distinct patterns than the ones that the simulation predicted. Some of these frequencies were similar, but some were noticeably different:

From the table you can see that 4 of the predictions were close, but 4 were also fairly off. It's not the case that there's some consistent amount of Hz that our predicted frequencies are off by compared to the simulated predictions. All of this leaves us with the question, what makes a certain frequency the most likely to be a natural frequency? and furthermore, are natural frequencies even giving the most distinct data? Using the microphone method, it seems like we generally got the best patterns, but our later results, particularly the 684.41 Hz result and the 724.19 Hz result got messy. The overall shape was still correct, but the nodal lines became a lot more difficult to make out. At one point we were testing to see if we could visually find a harmonic series, and in doing so, we found a very distinct pattern for 600 Hz, one that our simulation didn't predict. In our previous experiment, we found two distinct patterns at 604 Hz and 675 Hz that weren't predicted. This wasn't with as good of a setup, but generally the data still looked roughly like the simulations did, so this is still meaningful. In discussing the experimental results I was heavily focused on comparing to the simulations, however, this may have been a mistake. The simulation can either predict a natural frequency that didn't seem to exist, or miss a pattern that was very distinct. The latter could be explained by distinct patterns not always being right at the resonance frequency in some cases, though the former is more difficult to explain. I've realized in making all of these simulations that it would have been better to create contour plots instead. This way we could see how strong the vibration is meant to be at certain spots, and therefore how strong the nodal lines are likely to be at each location quantitatively. I didn't think that this would matter since at the predicted natural frequencies, we should be getting the strongest nodal lines anyways, but this doesn't seem to always be the case. It's also worth noting that in determining which natural modes to compare to, we basically just looked at which simulations seemed like they'd be most effectively produced via our center driven setup, but we could have mis-predicted. Perhaps the simulation was still useful when comparing our data to it where we already knew that we got a good pattern at that frequency, but using it to find what frequencies to use doesn't seem very effective. With all of this in mind, our most useful data set is the microphone data, with corresponding simulations that are still potentially useful, but should be used more as a tool to determine what the perfectly distinct pattern might look like around that particular frequency. One interesting pattern to consider is that our simulations tend to be the least accurate compared to the data as we get to the higher frequencies. This is most evident in the three examples I used previously in regards to distinct patterns that our simulations didn't predict. These values were all above 600 Hz.

# Experimental Issues
We improved a lot of the issues with our first setup when making the second, but there were still many possibilities for errors. The first problem is the fact that we're approximating our center driven force as a point force applied exactly at the center. The radius of the rod, the piece that connects through the plate is still much smaller than the side lengths of the plate, but it still has a meaningful radius. This causes the plates patterns to potentially form differently, and/or at different frequencies than is predicted in the simulation. Since the simulation uses the point force approximation. There's also an issue with how the rod attaches to the plate. Fixing the plate at the center with a rod and washer will resist some amount of bending. If there was a mode which had significant vibration at the center, since it's fixed there that part of the pattern might not show up at all. There was also a significant issue with the compression of the driver with our initial setup. We were using a pretty lightweight driver, and could visibly see that if we were holding the plate and the attachment up, then it wouldn't sink, but if we let it sit on it's own, then it would compress the driver a little bit. We used a more heavy duty driver for our final experimental setup, but this still could have been an issue to some degree. And this could weaken the vibrations, leading to worse patterns. This is likely why most of our patterns in our initial setup looked much messier than in our improved setup, though they still weren't perfect.

The most successful method we found in producing distinct patterns was the microphone method disucssed previously. However, you can see from our data that this method started to yield less distinct results at higher frequencies. Yet when we used higher frequencies determined through other methods or for other reasons, we still got distinct patterns at those frequencies, so clearly the experimental setup was still working fairly well, but our methods were flawed in this case. Another issue with the microphone is that we placed it at the center. This makes sense from a symmetry prespective, but if there's ever a pattern or part of a pattern which is mostly concentrated at the outside of the plate, then we might not get as strong of a response to it vs a pattern where it's mostly close to the center. And whenever you're trying to use a microphone there's also the issue of reflections throughout the room. We tried to combat this by putting some sound absorbant material hung from the ceiling some distance above the microphone, but this can only do so much. When going through the different frequencies we noticed possible harmonics by how distinct certain precise frequencies sounded, at regular integer multiples. However, we found that harmonics didn't give great patterns, and sometimes didn't give a pattern at all. But having these particular frequencies where we would sometimes get at least a decent looking pattern would make us think maybe it's a resonance, when in reality it was likely a harmonic. So it made finding the actual resonances more challenging, and we had to start accounting for these different integer possibitlies when evaluating if we thought a certain frequency was a resonance.

As for some more physical issues, I mentioned before how we had to use a level to make sure that everything was aligned properly. We made sure that this was the case at the start of each day we used the setup. However, we didn't re-level the plate between sets of data every time, and it did seem like it was slightly off in some cases. wiping off the plate, sugar being poured onto it over and over, among other possible reasons likely caused some minor misalignment issues. The sugar is another interesting source of error to investigate. As I noted in the setup section, we would remove the old then pour on new sugar each time we were taking data, and this helped a lot in regards to stopping the sugar from clumping, and making sure that we didn't lose too much sugar on the plate as the experiment went on. However, even in just one specific trial, there still would be an issue with clumping. There was a variance in how much vibration there was at different locations at the plate. So there could be an area where even though the pattern is supposed to form a certain way, since the vibration is so weak, the sugar clumping up at a large mass at those locations might have prevented the other sugar particles to properly form the shape they were supposed to. Not by the weakness of the vibration, but specifically by the mass of the other particles summing together locally and preventing movement. We also didn't have a particularly standardized method for determining how much sugar we were using. We would generally use half a tbsp, but there were some cases where we used less, sometimes more, and this could affect how strong the nodal lines looked at certain frequencies, as well as causing unwanted clumping.

There's a few more possible problems. The driver wasn't screwed into the wooden support, so there was a possiblity for some minor rattling. We were pretty sure that the aluminum we had was the same type that they used in the research paper so we used their same coefficients, but this could have been incorrect. The last fairly significant issue was the fact that the plate wasn't perfectly flat. It had more of a slight parabolic shape, that wasn't perfectly uniform either. It was more curved in one direction and a bit less in another. This could have caused various possible issues. We could get different nodal patterns, the lack of symmetry could distort some of the patterns, more sugar could fall off of certain sides compared to others, among other possibilites. There's also the issue with not being able to perfectly sweep the frequency range, having to rely on doing it incrementally as well as through other methods, but none of these methods were perfect. And finally, in hindsight we clearly lacked control variables. We should have measured the microphone distance, the amount of sugar we used each time, the specific settings of the amplifier, etc. Overall, there was a lot of room for error but we're still happy with how the experiment turned out.

# What to Improve/Investigate in the Future
The biggest improvement we'd want to make in the setup is using some sort of device which is able to measure the vibration of the plate directly, that way we wouldn't need to go through all this trouble to find at which frequencies we get the best responses. Along with this we'd want some sort of equipment that allows us to sweep frequency at a more granular level, without having to rely on using discrete increments. With these two improvements we could much more easily discover the exact frequencies which produce the most visually distinct patterns. We could also find a flatter plate, and this time ensure we know exactly which type of aluminum it is. In carrying out the measurements we'd also want to standardize things such as the amount of sugar, the driving amplitude, the exact microphone position, etc. In terms of the simulation, we'd create contour plots rather than traditional plots, that way we can predict how strong the specific nodal lines should be.

In terms of future investigations, there's a lot of different directions to go with this, but if wanting to keep a similar setup, we could spend more time in the higher frequencies we didn't work in much. The frequency range beyond ~700 Hz would be interesting to investigate, particularly because the patterns become more and more complex. Another thing to consider is the particular substance we're using to form the patterns. We used sugar, but there's no reason why we couldn't use sand, salt, or some other fine grain, non-sticky, light substance. I doubt that it would make a huge difference, but it could be an interesting way to investigate if perhaps there's any interesting quirks about sugar compared to the other possible substances that caused the data to turn out a particular way. Then just trying to eliminate as many of the issues raised in the previous sections as possible.

If wanting to change things up more, there's a lot of possible directions for that too. This whole experiment was based on math which applied nicely to this situation, but there's no reason why we couldn't try to model a different shape, one with nice symmetry like a circle, and perform an identical experiment. This way we could see the way the patterns change as the shape of the plate changes. Another variable to tweak could be the way that the plate is fixed. All of our math and simulations were based off of free edges and a fixed center, but we could try fixes some edges and having the center be free, fixing an edge with the center fixed too, etc. And finally, we could try driving the force off center instead, and see how that changes the patterns. Maybe we could use more than one driving force.
