# Experimental Setup
My goal is to create a thin elastic plate, so I want the thickness of the plate to be significantly smaller than the side lengths, and I want those side lengths to be equal to each other for symmetry reasons. The edges of the plate will be free, but I need a way to fix the plate at its center as well as vibrate it from there. I chose to use a driver to generate the vibrations. I attached a hollow disk to the dust cap, then ran a rod through it, which connected to the center of the plate on the other side. I want the radius of the rod to be very small relative to the area of the plate, so that the force applied can be effectively modeled as being concentrated at a point. I also want the rod to be as short as I can get it without causing the plate to impact the ring or dust cap, to reduce the possibility of bending. I used a short nylon screw to attach the plate to the ring. Next I made sure that the plate was level. I'd loosen the bolt holding the plate, use strong pieces of tape to make the plate level, and verify how level it is using a small level placed lightly on top of the plate after I screw the bolt back on again. Then once it was level in both directions, I placed it onto an enclosure. I created a box enclosure made of wood with 4 arms and a center hole to place the driver into. The driver was being held up on its edges such that its base wasn't touching anything. This way the driver wouldn't wobble. The enclosure was also useful such that I could use it to collect the extra sugar particles used during the experiment.

Now I need to find a source of different frequencies throughout whatever particular range is of interest, in my case 50-1000 Hz. I'll also need to sweep through these frequencies at as small increments as is feasible, with around 5 Hz increments being a good target. I used an analog function generator initially, but found it tedious to sweep through the frequency range, so I switched to a digital program. I plugged my source into an amplifier to get a stronger signal, then into the driver. While the plate was level, the room that I was testing in as well as the stool I used were slightly off, so I had to adjust the height of the stool slightly using little wedges. I also wanted to consider the SPL or SPdBV at different frequencies throughout the range, so I positioned a microphone a few inches above the center of the plate. My choice in centering the microphone was to ensure that regions on opposite sides of the plate equidistant from the center would have their contributions weighted approximately equally. I then connected the microphone to the same digital software I was going to use to generate frequencies. To record the visual data I placed a phone in a holder above the plate slightly off to the side. I used sugar to carry out the experiment, but any lightweight non-sticky fine-grain substance would work. Sand, silica sand, salt, baking soda, etc. Otherwise I just used a small air blowing device to quickly clean off the plate between uses, and some lights to make the visuals easier to see.

# Procedure
First verify that the plate is level, the microphone is centered, and that the digital/analog equipment is working properly. If your digital software allows, as both a test, and to be used for analysis, run a quick sweep through the entire 50-1000 Hz range, and save the image of the waveform. I will be sweeping the whole range, but by looking at the frequencies with the largest associated SPL or SPdBV (I'll use SPdBV going forwards since that's what I used), I can predict where at least some of the resonances are at. I begin by pouring sugar evenly onto the plate, I used half a tbsp at most, but sometimes less. Then I begin the frequency sweep at 50 Hz. I swept in increments of 5 Hz, but 10 Hz is sufficient as well, you just might miss some patterns. Usually patterns would begin shaping at a certain frequency, then at some point they'd reach their most distinct shape and structure, then they'd slowly form into a different shape. So I'm trying to figure out where each shape looks the most distinct. To do this I pay attention to the shapes that are being created by the sugar. Whenever it seems like I found a shape, I increment by a smaller amount till I think I found the most distinct pattern, determined by the SPdBV, how distinct the pattern looks, as well as how much the plate is vibrating visually. Once I have the exact frequency I think it's at, I blow/wipe off all of the sugar, turn off the sound, then pour on a fresh batch. Next I start a timelapse, and turn the sound back on. Once it looks like the sugar is done moving, or at least isn't going to move beyond whatever region it's staying in, I stop the video and record the frequency. I'll do this throughout the range, making sure that I at least hit every predicted resonance, and capture whatever else I find/interests me. In my case, I tried to find every new shape I could, test the frequencies predicted by my simulation, test some additional frequencies to investigate a possible harmonic, etc.

# Setup Details
My plate has sides 17" x 17", thickness 1/16", and is made of aluminum, but the specific type of aluminum I'm not sure. For calculations I just used the same constants for aluminum they used in the research paper.
I designed a cap for the ring in SolidWorks and created it on a CNC machine with guidance from my dad. It's a lightweight disk which fits into the ring, and is attached in place via epoxy. The disk holds the nylon screw in place which attaches to the plate. The reason the underside has a 6-way hollow pattern is simply because I wanted to reduce the weight while maintaining its structural strength. I figured any meaningless weight I can remove would be worthwhile so that the assembly doesn't weigh onto the driver too heavily, and cause it to overly compress.
To level the ring on the dust cap before attaching it via epoxy, I used the clamp setup shown in the image. I practiced leveling it beforehand, then used 15 minute epoxy to ensure I had enough time to get it just right before it hardened.
The tape I used to level the plate was a piece of Gorilla tape, and one smaller piece of regular blue tape. I attached it right next to the nylon screw hole.
